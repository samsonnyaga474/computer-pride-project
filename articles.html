5 IT Certifications That Will Boost Your Career in 2026

By the Computer Pride Team

The IT job market keeps shifting, and employers are getting more specific about what they want to see on a CV. A degree still matters, but a certification tells a hiring manager exactly what you can do right now, without them having to guess.

Cloud platforms remain at the top of the list. Certifications in AWS or Microsoft Azure show that you can actually deploy and manage systems in the real world, not just talk about them in theory. Even a foundational cloud certificate can open doors in companies that are moving their operations online.

Cybersecurity is close behind. As more businesses digitise, the risk of attacks grows with them, and organisations are actively hunting for people who understand how to secure a network. A certificate like CompTIA Security+ is often the first serious step into this field.

Project management certifications also continue to hold their value, especially for people who want to move from a technical role into leading a team. Being able to plan, budget and deliver a project on time is a skill that transfers across almost every industry.

Data-related certifications are becoming harder to ignore too. Businesses are collecting more information than ever, and they need people who can turn that data into decisions. A certificate in data analytics or a related tool can set you apart even without years of experience.

Finally, networking certifications still hold a steady place. Every organisation depends on a network that works, and the demand for people who can design, secure and troubleshoot one hasn't slowed down.

Whichever path fits you, the point is the same: a certification gives you proof, and proof gets you through the door faster. Computer Pride offers guided training across all five of these areas if you're ready to start.

Why NCC Education Qualifications Are Your Gateway to a UK Degree

By the Academic Team

Studying for a UK degree usually brings up two problems: cost and distance. Tuition and living expenses abroad add up fast, and not everyone can relocate for three or four years. NCC Education qualifications exist to solve exactly that problem.

NCC Education is a UK-based awarding body that partners with training centres like Computer Pride to deliver internationally recognised diplomas locally. You study the same syllabus, sit assessments set to the same standard, and end up with a qualification that UK universities already recognise.

The pathway typically starts with a Level 4 or Level 5 diploma in a subject such as Business or IT. Once that's complete, students can apply the credits toward the final year of a bachelor's degree with one of NCC's partner universities in the UK — either by transferring abroad for the last stage, or in some cases finishing the degree through distance learning.

For a lot of students, this route ends up being both cheaper and more manageable than starting a degree abroad from year one. You get time to build your English academic writing, adjust to degree-level study, and confirm the subject is right for you, all before making the bigger financial commitment of studying overseas.

It's also worth knowing that the qualification itself already carries weight with employers, even before it converts into a full degree. Businesses across East Africa recognise NCC diplomas as a mark of serious, internationally benchmarked study.

If a UK degree has felt out of reach, this is usually the most realistic way to get there without leaving Nairobi before you're ready.

Cloud Computing in Kenya: Why AWS and Azure Skills Are in High Demand

By James Mwangi

A few years ago, most Kenyan businesses kept their own physical servers on-site. That's changing quickly. More companies, from banks to logistics startups, are shifting their systems onto cloud platforms like Amazon Web Services (AWS) and Microsoft Azure, and that shift is creating real demand for people who know how to work with them.

The appeal for businesses is straightforward. Cloud infrastructure is cheaper to scale, easier to secure with the right setup, and doesn't require a company to buy and maintain its own hardware. But moving to the cloud isn't automatic — it needs people who understand how to migrate systems, manage storage, and keep costs under control once everything is live.

That's where the job market is heading. Roles like cloud support engineer, systems administrator and DevOps engineer increasingly list AWS or Azure certification as a requirement, not a bonus. Even IT roles that aren't cloud specific are starting to expect basic familiarity with at least one major platform.

For someone starting out, the good news is that both AWS and Azure offer structured certification paths. A foundational certificate builds the basics — how cloud services are billed, how storage and compute work, and how security is handled — before moving into more specialised tracks like networking, security or architecture.

Kenya's tech sector is still young enough that certified cloud professionals stand out clearly from the rest of the applicant pool. For IT students and working professionals alike, this is one of the more practical certifications to pick up right now, both for local opportunities and for roles with international companies hiring remotely from Kenya.

Cybersecurity Career Guide: From CompTIA Security+ to CISSP

By Sarah Kamau

Cybersecurity is one of the few IT fields where demand has consistently outpaced supply. Every organisation that runs on computers — which is basically every organisation now — needs someone watching for threats. The challenge for newcomers is knowing where to actually start.

The entry point most people take is CompTIA Security+. It covers the fundamentals: network security, threats and vulnerabilities, access control and basic risk management. It doesn't assume prior security experience, which makes it a realistic starting certificate for someone moving into cybersecurity from a general IT background.

After Security+, most people specialise. Some move toward ethical hacking and penetration testing, learning how attackers think so they can find weaknesses before someone else does. Others lean into security operations — monitoring systems, responding to incidents, and keeping networks running safely day to day.

Mid-career, certifications like CompTIA CySA+ or Certified Ethical Hacker (CEH) build on that specialisation with more hands-on, scenario-based skills. These are usually pursued once someone has a year or two of practical experience behind them.

At the top of the ladder sits CISSP — the Certified Information Systems Security Professional certification. It's aimed at experienced professionals moving into senior or leadership security roles, and it typically requires several years of verified work experience before you can even sit the exam. It's less about hands-on technical tasks and more about designing and managing security at an organisational level.

The path from Security+ to CISSP isn't a quick one, and it isn't meant to be. But each step along the way is a real, employable milestone on its own — you don't need to reach the top to start building a solid cybersecurity career.

Short Courses vs Professional Certifications: Which Is Right for You?

By the Training Advisory Team

This is one of the most common questions we get from people walking through our doors: should I take a short course, or go for a full professional certification? The honest answer depends on what you're trying to achieve, and how much time you have to get there.

Short courses are built for speed. They usually run for a few days to a few weeks and focus on one practical skill — things like Excel for business, basic web design, or an introduction to a specific software tool. If you need a skill quickly for a job you already have, or you're testing whether a field interests you before committing further, a short course is the lower-risk option.

Professional certifications are a bigger commitment. They go deeper into a subject, often require passing a formal exam set by an international body, and carry more weight with employers because they're independently verified. They take longer and typically cost more, but the certificate becomes a permanent credential on your CV, not just a completed workshop.

A useful way to think about it: short courses are good for adding a specific tool to your toolkit, while certifications are good for building a career direction. Someone who already has a job and needs to pick up a new skill fast will usually lean toward a short course. Someone planning a career move into IT, cybersecurity or cloud computing will get more long-term value from a recognised certification.

It's also worth knowing these two options aren't mutually exclusive. A lot of our students start with a short course to confirm they enjoy a subject, then move on to the full certification once they're confident it's the right path.

If you're not sure which route fits your situation, our training advisors are happy to talk through your goals and timeline before you commit to either one.
